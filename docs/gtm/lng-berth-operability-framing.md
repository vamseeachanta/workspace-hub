# LNG / FSRU Berth Operability Framing

Purpose: give ACE a buyer-usable, engineering-first way to discuss LNG and FSRU berth operability without over-claiming full terminal design scope or quoting unsupported criteria.

## What berth operability means in LNG / FSRU work

In LNG and FSRU work, berth operability means the practical ability to bring a vessel in, keep it safely alongside, transfer cargo within ship/shore interface limits, and depart when needed under the site's real environmental and harbour conditions.

This is not just a question of whether the berth structure survives extreme loading. It is the combined performance of:
- metocean forcing at the berth and approach
- harbour or basin response, including long-period effects where relevant
- vessel motions alongside
- mooring loads and restoring behaviour
- fender reactions and stand-off behaviour
- transfer-system motion and geometry limits
- navigation, tug, pilot, and marine-control constraints
- operating procedures for normal, degraded, and emergency states

For FSRU projects, operability usually becomes even more central because the marine interface is not a one-off ship call. The terminal depends on a floating asset, visiting LNG carriers, and shore-side sendout or utility interfaces all remaining workable as one system.

## Why operability often matters more commercially than simple survival design

A berth can be structurally credible and still be commercially weak.

Clients are usually not buying only a berth that can survive a design storm. They are buying a marine interface that can reliably support cargo transfer, schedule adherence, and terminal throughput. In practice, lost operability can drive project economics harder than rare ultimate-limit events because it affects:
- how often carriers can berth and stay on schedule
- how often cargo transfer has to be delayed, suspended, or stopped
- whether forecast windows are long enough to complete transfer safely
- tug and pilot utilization
- FSRU sendout continuity and inventory management
- contractual exposure from downtime, congestion, and demurrage

That is why LNG berth discussions often need to move quickly from "will it survive?" to "when is it actually usable?"

## Main drivers of operability

### 1. Metocean
Key drivers include the local combination of wave climate, long-period swell where present, wind, current, tide, and water-level variation. Directionality matters as much as magnitude because the berth and harbour geometry can make one sector much more limiting than another.

### 2. Harbour response
At many LNG sites, offshore conditions alone do not explain berth behaviour. Wave transformation, basin agitation, long-period oscillation, and local resonance can amplify motions at the berth and reduce usable uptime. This is often where a seemingly acceptable exposure becomes an operability problem.

### 3. Mooring loads
Operability is often governed by dynamic line loads, not just static arrangement. Surge, sway, yaw, low-frequency drift response, and line stiffness all affect whether the vessel can remain controlled alongside. For LNG and FSRU interfaces, the mooring system is part of the transfer safety case, not a separate marine detail.

### 4. Fender reactions
Fender behaviour influences stand-off distance, vessel offset, reaction loads, and local berth response. A berth may appear stable overall but still become limited by unacceptable fender compression, reaction, or vessel position relative to the jetty and transfer equipment.

### 5. Transfer envelope
Cargo transfer usually has tighter limits than berth survival. The governing issue is the relative position and motion between the vessel manifold and the shore or floating transfer connection. Loading arms, hoses, emergency release arrangements, and manifold geometry can all become the limiting condition before the berth itself reaches a structural limit.

### 6. Navigation and tug constraints
Safe operability starts before first line ashore and continues until the vessel is clear. Approach alignment, tug control, turning constraints, current across the berth, channel restrictions, and pilotage rules can all create a practical weather limit that is lower than the pure alongside limit.

### 7. Procedures and decision rules
A technically sound berth can still perform poorly if operating states are not clearly separated. Berthing, connection, steady transfer, suspension, disconnection, and departure need explicit decision logic tied to marine conditions and forecast windows.

## Distinguish the limit states

A useful client conversation separates four different limit states rather than collapsing everything into one generic weather limit.

### Berthing
The limit for approach, tug control, contact with fenders, and making fast. This is often governed by manoeuvring and harbour conditions, not just the eventual alongside response.

### Alongside
The limit for remaining safely moored at berth. This is where vessel motions, line loads, offsets, and fender reactions are checked as an integrated marine system.

### Cargo-transfer
The limit for connecting and maintaining transfer without exceeding the ship/shore motion and geometry envelope. In many LNG cases this is the most restrictive operating state.

### Departure / emergency unberthing
The limit for disconnecting and clearing the berth in a worsening forecast or abnormal condition. This matters because a berth concept is weaker if the vessel can enter and transfer in marginal conditions but cannot leave safely when conditions deteriorate.

## What ACE can screen now vs what requires deeper project-specific studies

### What ACE can screen now
ACE can support early-stage screening and buyer-side engineering framing when the client needs to know whether a berth concept looks directionally workable, where the likely constraints sit, and what data gaps matter most.

That screening can include:
- identifying the likely governing operability drivers for an LNG import, export, bunkering, STS, or FSRU interface
- separating the problem into berthing, alongside, transfer, and departure limit states
- reviewing whether the berth concept is likely to be governed by harbour response, mooring behaviour, transfer envelope, navigation constraints, or procedural gaps
- checking whether the available metocean, vessel, and berth information is sufficient for a credible operability basis
- framing a screening-level mooring, fendering, and ship/shore interface assessment
- comparing terminal archetypes or concept options at a high level
- turning a vague client concern into a scoped study brief for deeper analysis

This matches ACE's positioning as an engineering bridge between offshore mooring and marine-operations analysis and LNG terminal decision support.

### What requires deeper project-specific studies
Detailed operability statements require project data and project criteria. Deeper work is needed when the client needs defensible operating limits, equipment compatibility confirmation, or investment-grade confidence.

That usually means project-specific work such as:
- site metocean definition and directional joint-condition development
- wave transformation, harbour agitation, or basin-response studies
- dynamic vessel-motion, mooring-load, and fender-reaction analysis for defined vessel classes and berth layouts
- loading-arm or hose envelope checks using equipment-specific limits
- navigation simulation, tug assessment, and marine-operations procedure development
- downtime and throughput modelling linked to forecast windows and operational rules
- FSRU-specific interface studies where sendout continuity, stationed-unit response, and visiting-carrier transfer logic are coupled

ACE should not imply that a generic screen can replace those studies.

## Typical questions a client is actually asking

Clients rarely ask for "berth operability" in abstract terms. They are usually asking one or more of these:
- Is this berth concept likely to be workable for the vessel classes we expect?
- Will the berth be limited by motions, mooring loads, fenders, or transfer equipment?
- Are we dealing with a survival-design problem or an uptime problem?
- Is harbour response likely to be the hidden issue at this site?
- Can an FSRU or LNG carrier stay safely alongside often enough to support the business case?
- What conditions are likely to stop cargo transfer before they stop simple berthing?
- Can we safely disconnect and depart if conditions worsen?
- What minimum data do we need before paying for a full operability study?
- Is the current terminal concept missing something obvious in marine operations or interface design?
- What follow-on studies are actually worth funding next?

## Minimal input set needed to do useful screening

ACE can do useful screening once the client can provide a disciplined minimum input set. That normally includes:
- site location and terminal concept description
- berth type and basic layout, including orientation and whether the case is jetty, dolphin berth, side-by-side, or FSRU-related
- intended vessel classes and any known principal particulars relevant to the interface
- available metocean information for the approach and berth area
- any known harbour, breakwater, basin, or local exposure constraints
- mooring concept if already defined, or at least intended berthing arrangement
- fender concept if known
- transfer concept: loading arms, hoses, side-by-side transfer, or other ship/shore interface arrangement
- operating mode: import, export, bunkering, STS, FSRU unloading, or combined service
- known operational constraints such as tug availability, pilotage rules, channel restrictions, or mandatory departure logic
- the client's actual decision need: concept selection, risk screen, pre-FEED challenge, lender-style review, or operations troubleshooting

If some of these are missing, ACE can still help by identifying which missing inputs are truly gating and which can be bounded for a preliminary screen.

## Boundaries / non-claims

ACE should stay explicit about scope boundaries.

This framing note does not claim that ACE can, from limited public information alone:
- define project operating limits without project-specific criteria and analysis
- quote universal numerical acceptance criteria for LNG or FSRU berth operability
- confirm loading-arm or hose suitability without vendor and project interface data
- replace harbour studies, dynamic mooring analysis, navigation simulation, or detailed terminal design
- certify compliance with a terminal's full regulatory or class basis from screening work alone

Conference programmes and general industry topic signals may justify why the topic matters commercially, but they are not engineering evidence for project decisions. Engineering claims must come back to site data, vessel particulars, berth geometry, recognised guidance, and project-specific analysis.

## Suggested follow-on studies when screening finds risk

When screening shows credible risk or major uncertainty, the usual next steps are:
- metocean data consolidation and directional criteria development
- harbour agitation, wave-transformation, or basin-response study
- dynamic vessel-motion, mooring-load, and fender-reaction assessment
- transfer-envelope study using the intended loading-arm or hose system
- approach, berthing, and departure assessment including tug and pilot constraints
- procedure development for cargo stop, disconnect, and emergency-unberthing logic
- downtime and throughput assessment tied to marine limits and forecast windows
- FSRU-specific interface study covering stationed-unit response, visiting-carrier transfer, and sendout continuity interactions
