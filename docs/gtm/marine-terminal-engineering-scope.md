# Marine Terminal Engineering Scope Note

Purpose: define an engineering-first ACE marine terminal scope grounded in the current GTM positioning and wiki synthesis, and reusable as source material for later website, proposal, and screening-packet work.

## 1. Why Marine Terminal Engineering Is Relevant to ACE

Marine terminal engineering is a credible ACE adjacency because the first practical client questions sit at the same interface ACE already understands well:

- vessel motions and operability limits
- mooring load paths and stationkeeping logic at berth
- fender reactions and berth contact behavior
- ship/shore transfer-interface constraints
- weather-limited marine operations and decision support
- screening of whether a terminal concept is directionally workable before deeper design

This is not a claim that ACE is a full terminal EPC or port masterplanning firm. The relevant transfer is narrower and more useful: offshore mooring, marine operations, operability framing, and interface analysis map directly into berth operability and LNG marine-terminal questions.

That positioning is already consistent with current GTM documents:

- `docs/gtm/core-engineering-work-conversion.md` defines Workstream 5 as a Marine Terminal Engineering Starter Packet focused on berth operability and ship/shore transfer interfaces
- `docs/gtm/capability-map.md` lists Marine Terminal Engineering (LNG / Port Ops) as an available capability around berth operability, mooring/fendering, transfer interface, harbour response, and FSRU/import-export terminal screening
- `docs/gtm/client-conversion-pipeline.md` already includes a marine terminal service line and buyer segment for LNG terminal / FSRU / port-operations teams
- `knowledge/wikis/marine-engineering/wiki/concepts/lng-marine-terminal-engineering.md` provides the current terminal-systems synthesis

## 2. Near-Term ACE Engineering Boundary

ACE's near-term marine terminal position should be defined as screening, framing, and decision-support work around the berth and ship/shore interface, not full terminal design EPC work.

ACE can credibly position near-term work as:

- berth operability and workability framing
- LNG carrier / import berth / export jetty / FSRU / bunkering / STS interface screening
- mooring and fender load-path review at berth
- transfer envelope review for loading arms, hoses, manifolds, and emergency release assumptions
- harbour-response and exposed-versus-sheltered berth implications at screening level
- navigation and marine-operations constraints that affect berthing, unberthing, and transfer uptime
- technical review of whether a concept is mature enough to proceed into FEED, detailed design, or specialist simulation

ACE should not claim, at this stage:

- full terminal EPC design
- detailed civil/structural jetty, dolphin, breakwater, or revetment design
- complete navigation simulation campaigns or pilotage validation as a standalone delivered specialty
- final hazardous area classification, QRA, or full process-safety design package
- final loading-arm design or vendor-certified transfer-system design
- final signoff of terminal design without project-specific data, terminal criteria, and deeper references

The boundary must remain explicit: ACE is strongest at the marine engineering interface where vessel response, mooring/fendering, transfer limits, and operating philosophy meet. Full terminal design still requires broader civil, structural, process, E&I, safety, regulatory, and local-port inputs.

## 3. What ACE Can Do Now

Grounded in the current capability map, marine-ops positioning, and wiki synthesis, ACE can lead with the following now.

### 3.1 Berth operability screening

ACE can perform early-stage framing around:

- what is likely to govern first at berth: vessel motions, mooring loads, fender reactions, transfer envelope, or harbour response
- whether a berth concept appears directionally workable for a stated vessel class and operating philosophy
- what environmental directions, periods, or low-frequency effects are most likely to close the berth early
- where the concept needs deeper study before a buyer should rely on it

### 3.2 Mooring and fendering interface review

ACE can assess at screening level:

- mooring arrangement logic and line-function balance
- likely load-path issues across breast, spring, headline, and stern lines
- fender duty logic, berth contact assumptions, and vessel motion compatibility
- whether exposed or long-period response is likely to create unacceptable line or fender behavior
- where berth hardware and dolphin loads need specialist detailed follow-on checks

### 3.3 Transfer-interface framing

ACE can review:

- loading-arm or hose envelope as an operating constraint rather than a purely mechanical item
- manifold and connection-point compatibility assumptions
- relative-motion sensitivities between vessel and terminal connection point
- cargo stop, alarm, disconnect, and emergency release triggers at screening level
- differences in interface logic between export, import/regas, FSRU, STS, and bunkering cases

### 3.4 Marine operations and navigation screening

ACE can frame:

- berthing, unberthing, tug, and weather-window logic
- exposed versus sheltered terminal implications for marine operations
- simultaneous-operations constraints relevant to transfer, bunkering, or STS activity
- whether approach/departure constraints are likely to dominate before alongside criteria do

### 3.5 Technical-assurance and buyer support

ACE can support:

- pre-FEED or diligence review of whether the marine-terminal concept is directionally sound
- early identification of data gaps that make current conclusions weak
- decision-grade explanation of what has been screened, what remains uncertain, and what should trigger escalation
- translation of marine engineering issues into buyer-facing go/no-go, risk, and next-step language

## 4. Typical Terminal Archetypes ACE Can Discuss Credibly

ACE should treat marine terminals as multiple distinct operating problems, not one generic port-services claim.

### 4.1 LNG export jetty

Typical focus:

- carrier motions at exposed or semi-exposed jetty heads
- loading-arm envelope and transfer uptime
- line balance and fender duty for frequent loading calls
- approach/departure and tug philosophy

### 4.2 LNG import / regas berth

Typical focus:

- receiving-terminal berth operability for LNG carriers
- unloading limits tied to vessel motions and transfer interface
- weather and harbour-response effects on availability
- berth concept comparison before detailed development

### 4.3 FSRU-based receiving terminal

Typical focus:

- permanently or semi-permanently moored FSRU interface questions
- side-by-side or dedicated berth transfer logic where relevant
- operability under broader environmental exposure than sheltered conventional jetties
- interaction between terminal uptime, marine systems, and transfer philosophy

### 4.4 Ship-to-ship transfer arrangement

Typical focus:

- relative-motion and interface control between two floating bodies
- mooring arrangement and fender management between vessels
- transfer-hose envelope and emergency disconnect philosophy
- weather and manoeuvring constraints during setup and transfer

### 4.5 LNG bunkering berth or transfer location

Typical focus:

- smaller-scale but tighter interface tolerances
- compatibility of receiving and supplying vessel motions
- procedural constraints, simultaneous operations, and port-control logic
- whether harbour, passing-vessel, or local wake effects dominate the operating limit

These are screening-level discussion archetypes. They are not substitutes for project-specific design packages.

## 5. Core Engineering Subsystems

The current wiki synthesis supports a consistent subsystem view. ACE should use the following six-part stack in proposals and website derivations.

### 5.1 Berth / jetty system

Key questions:

- berth orientation relative to wind, wave, swell, current, and passing traffic
- berth, dolphin, and jetty-head arrangement
- exposed versus sheltered response characteristics
- under-keel, water-level, and approach geometry constraints

### 5.2 Mooring / fendering system

Key questions:

- line arrangement, pretension philosophy, and line-function balance
- low-frequency drift, resonance, and line-load amplification
- fender reaction, deflection, and vessel-contact compatibility
- snap-back, fatigue, wear, and reserve-margin concerns

### 5.3 Transfer interface

Key questions:

- loading-arm or hose geometry and reach envelope
- manifold alignment and connection-point compatibility
- allowable relative motions during transfer
- alarm, cargo-stop, and emergency release thresholds

### 5.4 Operability / workability

Key questions:

- downtime drivers and environmental limits
- long-period swell, seiche, harbour agitation, or low-frequency response
- what closes the berth first and under which operating state
- how marine criteria map into throughput and availability consequences

### 5.5 Navigation / marine operations

Key questions:

- approach, turning, berthing, tug, and departure constraints
- restricted-weather criteria and marine-control philosophy
- sequencing and simultaneous operations
- whether marine access governs before alongside transfer criteria do

### 5.6 Safety / isolation / emergency systems

Key questions:

- ship/shore ESD assumptions and interface hierarchy
- emergency release and disconnect logic
- exclusion-zone and abnormal-operations implications at the berth
- coordination between terminal, pilot, tug, and marine control functions

## 6. Mandatory Project Inputs for Real Work

Real terminal work is only defensible when the project input basis is explicit. At minimum, ACE should ask for the following before moving beyond framing-level discussion.

### 6.1 Vessel particulars

- vessel type and operating mode
- principal dimensions, displacement, drafts, manifold location, and loading condition range
- windage and relevant motion-response basis if available
- mooring arrangement, winch philosophy, and line characteristics
- fender-contact geometry and operational constraints

### 6.2 Berth geometry and arrangement

- berth orientation and layout
- dolphin spacing, jetty-head geometry, and clearances
- water depth, under-keel basis, and tidal/water-level range
- breakwater, basin, or local harbour arrangement influencing response

### 6.3 Metocean and harbour-response basis

- site wind, wave, swell, current, and tide data
- directional and spectral basis, including long-period content where relevant
- harbour agitation, resonance, or seiche information if available
- passing-vessel or wake environment where relevant

### 6.4 Transfer-system limits

- loading-arm or hose type and envelope limits
- allowable motions or manifold-relative displacements
- emergency release assumptions
- cargo transfer, cooldown, purge, disconnect, and shutdown criteria

### 6.5 Terminal criteria and operating philosophy

- recognised terminal criteria, owner criteria, and governing references
- allowable downtime, throughput target, and operating-state definitions
- berthing/unberthing rules, tug philosophy, and pilotage assumptions
- alarm, stop-transfer, disconnect, and unberthing decision logic

### 6.6 Hardware and design-basis information

- fender selection basis and performance curves if available
- mooring hardware capacities and arrangement
- dolphin or berth load limits if already defined
- known vendor constraints, class requirements, or local-port rules

Without these inputs, ACE can still do framing and gap-definition work, but should not imply project-specific conclusions are reliable.

## 7. Appropriate Evidence Hierarchy

ACE should keep the evidence hierarchy explicit in every marine-terminal discussion.

### 7.1 Topic signals

Useful for theme selection and GTM prioritization, but not design evidence:

- conference programmes such as LNG2026
- public agenda topics about bunkering, STS, marine terminal operations, or FSRUs
- general market commentary showing where buyer attention is moving

These sources justify that the topic is live. They do not justify design claims.

### 7.2 Engineering synthesis sources

Useful for structuring the problem and defining the subsystem stack:

- current wiki synthesis such as `knowledge/wikis/marine-engineering/wiki/concepts/lng-marine-terminal-engineering.md`
- operational framing documents such as SIGTTO-style marine terminal guidance
- incident-driven lessons such as LNG terminal mooring-failure references

These support engineering framing, questions, and work-package structure.

### 7.3 Design evidence

Required for real project deliverables:

- project-specific vessel data
- berth geometry and terminal arrangement
- metocean and harbour-response data
- transfer-system vendor limits and operating criteria
- recognised references such as PIANC, OCIMF, SIGTTO, and project-specific terminal criteria
- site-specific analyses, calculations, and simulations tied to the actual operating basis

The rule is simple: LNG2026 and similar pages are topic signals only. Real design or decision-support deliverables must rest on project data and recognised engineering references.

## 8. Buyer-Facing Engineering Outputs

ACE's marine-terminal offering should convert into clear buyer-facing outputs rather than broad capability claims.

Typical outputs include:

- berth operability framing note
- marine-terminal screening memo for a stated vessel and berth concept
- mooring / fender / transfer-interface constraint register
- data-gap and escalation memo for pre-FEED or diligence teams
- concept-comparison matrix across terminal archetypes or berth arrangements
- risk-ranked list of what likely governs uptime and what needs deeper study
- owner or investor technical-assurance note explaining what is screened versus unresolved

These outputs are decision-support deliverables. They help a buyer decide whether to refine, re-scope, or escalate. They are not substitutes for full FEED or detailed design packages.

## 9. Assumptions and Limitations

ACE should state the following assumptions and limitations clearly.

- Near-term marine terminal work is screening, framing, and technical-assurance oriented.
- Results are only as good as the vessel, berth, metocean, and transfer criteria provided.
- Operability conclusions are especially sensitive to long-period response, harbour effects, and transfer-system motion limits.
- Exposed terminal behavior, FSRU cases, STS transfers, and bunkering interfaces often require more detailed follow-on work than first assumed.
- Detailed civil/structural, process, safety, navigation-simulation, and regulatory work remains outside the default ACE front-line scope unless separately partnered or specifically scoped.
- Public topic pages and conference programmes do not constitute design evidence.

## 10. Can-Say-Now / Cannot-Claim-Yet

### Can say now

- ACE can support screening-level berth operability, mooring/fendering, and transfer-interface studies.
- ACE can help compare export, import, FSRU, bunkering, and STS marine-interface archetypes at early stage.
- ACE can identify what is likely to govern the berth first and what project inputs are still missing.
- ACE can provide buyer-facing engineering framing for terminal-adjacent questions without pretending to deliver full EPC design.
- ACE can use marine-ops and mooring experience to support terminal decision support and scope definition.

### Cannot claim yet

- ACE should not claim complete terminal design capability across all disciplines.
- ACE should not imply conference themes or LNG2026 pages are design-data evidence.
- ACE should not imply final operability conclusions without vessel particulars, berth geometry, metocean basis, and transfer limits.
- ACE should not claim full loading-arm design, final navigation validation, or full process-safety design as default in-house scope.
- ACE should not present screening work as equivalent to class-approved, FEED-complete, or EPC-ready design.

## 12. Summary Position

ACE should position marine terminal engineering as a disciplined marine-interface offering: berth operability, mooring/fendering logic, transfer-envelope screening, harbour-response awareness, and marine-operations decision support.

That is broad enough to matter commercially and narrow enough to be defensible.

The critical message is not "ACE does ports." The critical message is that ACE can help terminal, LNG, and FSRU teams answer early marine-interface questions honestly, identify what really governs operability, and define when a concept is ready for deeper specialist design.
